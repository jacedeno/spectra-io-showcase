Six Months Later: What Production Taught Me About My Industrial Vibration Pipeline

In February I published an architecture and promised dashboards. Then the plant, the cellular bill, and a 19-hour outage rewrote three of my decisions.

Six months ago I published an article here describing how I built an industrial vibration monitoring pipeline with open standards and no vendor lock-in: wireless sensors, MQTT, a time-series database, and a plan for dashboards and alerting.

The pipeline was real. Everything after "future plans" was not, yet.

It is now. The platform runs in production at an operating cement plant — crushers, vane compressors, gearboxes, motors — streaming over 4G, serving two organizations from one codebase. And along the way I had to change three of the decisions I published, because a live fleet argues back.

Here is what changed, what broke, and what I would tell anyone building the same thing.

First, the part I did not change

The sensors. The edge is CTC Connect equipment — WS300 triaxial and WS200 single-axis dynamic sensors, WS100 process sensors, and an ACCESS360 gateway — and it carries the parts of the problem software cannot solve: an industrial accelerometer that survives cement dust and years on a battery, and a radio that gets a 25,600-sample capture off a machine without a cable.

Everything I describe below is software standing on that. Choosing hardware that reports honestly and lets you configure it remotely is what made the rest possible.

Change 1: the low-code flow became a service

I built the first pipeline in Node-RED. It was the right call to prove the concept — you can see the whole flow, and you are writing MQTT parsers in minutes.

Then it hit its ceiling. The flow lives inside its own container, not in version control, so "what changed last Tuesday" has no answer. It was hardcoded to one gateway. And it was not the tool I wanted computing an FFT on every reading.

It is now a small Python service: one job, one container. It takes messages off the broker, reassembles the ones the gateway split in transit, translates the vendor's field names into ours, tags every point with gateway and sensor, and writes. It listens with a wildcard across gateways, so a new gateway is a new tag value, not a new deployment. It has a test suite. Every change is a reviewable diff.

That last property is the one that matters. Two process sensors were silently invisible for weeks because one message channel nests its fields one level deeper than the rest, and a flat-only parser accepted the message, found nothing, and dropped it. No crash, no error — just missing data. What surfaced it was a per-channel message counter on a dashboard. What made the fix safe was a test that reproduces the payload.

Change 2: the spectrum moved, and then had to shrink

In February I described the sensors doing the FFT. Today the service computes it.

The reason is bandwidth arithmetic. The raw waveform already crosses the cellular link on every full reading, so computing the spectrum from it costs nothing extra. Asking the sensor for a spectrum means paying for the same information twice, on a metered SIM.

Then storage pushed back. A full-resolution spectrum plus the raw waveform serializes to about 977 KB, and the database rejects any single string field over 64 KB. The write fails — but only partially. The small scalar row still lands. So the symptom is not "no data", it is "trends fine, spectra mysteriously absent", which is a much worse thing to debug.

The naive fix is to squash the spectrum to a fixed number of bins. I tried it, and it quietly ruined the product: a reading degraded from its native 2.06 Hz to about 3.08 Hz per point — 185 CPM. Fine for a trend line. Useless for bearing analysis on an 800 RPM machine, where the frequencies you are hunting sit below a few hundred Hz.

What ships now is a dual-band reduction. Every native bin at or below 1 kHz is stored untouched — 0.51 Hz, or 31 CPM per bin. Only the band above is bucketed, and with a peak-preserving maximum rather than an average, so a high-frequency defect tone still survives. The waveform is stored as a min/max envelope that keeps the exact extremes, so the peak and peak-to-peak numbers stay correct. Band features are computed before any reduction, so the values an alarm fires on are never the downsampled ones.

Six times finer where the diagnosis lives, and it fits in the field limit.

The lesson: "downsample to fit" is not one decision. Where you spend the resolution is the decision.

Change 3: the database tier

The February article named InfluxDB 3 Core, and I meant it — SQL over a columnar store, open format, no proprietary query language.

It ran into two ceilings on real data: a cap on databases, and a per-query limit on the number of Parquet files, which a long trend window crosses once you have months of history. Moving to the Enterprise tier removed both, mostly because it compacts automatically.

Worth being explicit about the trade: that tier is free for non-commercial use and limited to two CPU cores. It is the right call for a beta and an open decision for V1.0 — a commercial license, or moving the telemetry sink onto the Postgres cluster that already holds the app's relational state. Publishing an architecture does not mean pretending every choice is permanent.

The 19-hour outage

This is the part I would want to read.

The database crash-looped for 19 hours and lost the telemetry that arrived during it. 3,940 restarts, each about ten seconds, never binding its port. The log said the node was "still running in another process". There was no other process.

The cause was not a power cut. The database needs about 19 seconds to stop cleanly — flush the buffer, checkpoint, close the write-ahead log. Docker's default stop grace period is 10 seconds. So on any host shutdown, including a perfectly clean one, it gets killed mid-write and leaves a zero-byte WAL file behind. On the next start, replay skips the empty file, the server then creates the next segment with create-if-not-exists semantics, collides with the empty file already holding that number, and reads the collision as another process owning the node.

One config line I had never thought about turned every reboot into a coin flip.

Two things turned a bad restart into a 19-hour outage, and both were mine:

The ingester had nowhere to put readings. The MQTT client acknowledges a message as soon as the handler returns. A reading not persisted at that instant is gone — the broker will not redeliver it. Failed writes were dropped on the floor, politely. There is now an on-disk spool: a failed batch is parked instead of raising, new batches queue behind the backlog so ordering survives, batches are fsynced and atomically renamed so a torn write is impossible, and the spool is bounded so a long outage costs the oldest readings rather than the disk the database lives on.

Nothing paged me. This is the real lesson. An error rule was firing the entire 19 hours. It turned red on a screen nobody was looking at, because the stack had no alert router configured. Detection without routing is not monitoring.

Alerting is now three layers, each covering the blind spot of the one above it: rules that watch the services and push to a phone; a probe on separate hardware, because a watcher that lives on the machine it watches dies with it; and an external dead-man's switch that alerts on the absence of a heartbeat, because nothing inside the house can report that the house is dark.

A detail worth stealing: "no data" is a per-rule decision, not a default. Alert when absence is the failure. Stay silent everywhere else — a counter has no series until it first increments, and per-sensor gauges vanish across a restart. Left at the default, those rules page you on every deploy, and an alert you learn to ignore is worse than no alert.

The one-line option that almost invalidated everything

After shipping the dual-band spectrum I opened the app and measured about 90 CPM on screen. The stored data was correct at 31.

The charting library was decimating the series to the canvas pixel width — roughly every third of 4,000 points — through a "sampling" option that exists precisely to make dense charts fast. It is on by default in plenty of examples. It renders beautifully. And it silently throws away the exact thing an analyst is looking for.

Pixel sampling is now banned on every diagnostic chart in this codebase. Canvas draws the full 4,000-point series without complaining.

If you build anything where the shape of the data is the product, go read your chart config today.

Where it is now

A reliability engineer opens one URL and gets the fleet as their plant is organised — site, group, assembly, asset, monitoring point — with trend, spectrum and waveform per sensor. Enter the running speed and the bearing geometry and the platform draws where BPFO, BPFI, BSF and FTF should appear. Thresholds per indicator, alerts with acknowledge/assign/resolve and an audit trail, CSV export, and the same thing on a phone next to the machine.

No inbound port is open at the plant or at the platform. Nothing plugs into the plant network. The whole on-site footprint is a sensor on a machine and a box on a wall.
